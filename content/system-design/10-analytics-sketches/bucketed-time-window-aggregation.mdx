## Scanning raw events for every chart does not scale

A dashboard that asks "requests per minute for service X over the last 24 hours" can be answered by filtering a raw event table. At low volume that is fine. At millions of events per minute it becomes a full scan dressed as a product feature — every refresh pays for the same aggregation again, and ad-hoc filters multiply the cost.

The alternative is to decide the time grain up front, fold events into that grain as they arrive (or in a near-line job), and store one row — or one sketch — per `(metric_key, bucket_start)`. A day at one-minute grain is 1,440 buckets; a chart is a sum or merge across a contiguous range of those buckets, not across every raw event.

## Pick a grain, then commit to how buckets close

<Steps>
  <Step title="Choose the finest grain you will query interactively">
    Product graphs often want one-minute or five-minute buckets. Alerting may want ten-second buckets for a few hours of retention. Finer grain means more rows and more merge work; coarser grain means you cannot zoom without going back to raw events. Store raw (or sampled) events for re-aggregation; serve interactive queries from buckets — 10.07 Raw Events Vs Derived Analytics.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assign each event to a bucket by event time">
    `bucket = floor(event_timestamp / bucket_width) * bucket_width`. Use event time for analytics truth; processing time drifts with consumer lag and makes the same incident look like it happened when the job caught up. Late events need a policy: update the old bucket within a grace period, or spill to a correction stream.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Aggregate inside the open bucket">
    Maintain in-memory counters, HLLs, Count-Min Sketches, or t-digests keyed by business dimensions for the current bucket. On each event, update the structure for that key. This is the same hot-path idea as 10.02 View Counting At Scale, scoped to a time interval that will close.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Seal the bucket and write an immutable aggregate">
    When wall clock (plus allowed lateness) passes the bucket end, flush the aggregate to durable storage and start a new empty structure. Sealed buckets are cheap to cache and to merge upward (twenty 1-minute HLLs merge into a 20-minute HLL — 10.05 Mergeable Sketches For Analytics).
  </Step>
</Steps>

Sliding windows ("last 15 minutes continuously") are usually implemented as a ring of fine buckets you sum on read, not as one structure that deletes arbitrary old events. Deleting from a Bloom filter or HLL is painful; dropping a whole bucket is trivial.

<Callout type="note">
Tumbling windows (aligned to clock boundaries) and sliding windows (every query shifts) are different UX, same storage trick: keep fine buckets, assemble the window at query time. Sliding does not require a different sketch type — it requires enough retained fine grain to cover the slide.
</Callout>

## Late data and the grace period

Out-of-order events are normal once you have multiple producers and a queue. A strict seal-at-boundary rule undercounts whenever an event arrives after flush. Common pattern: keep the bucket writable for a grace period (30 seconds to a few minutes, depending on SLA), accept updates to already-"closed" buckets, and emit a revised aggregate version. Downstream readers either wait for watermark progress or show a provisional number marked as such. Systems that pretend lateness does not exist show mysterious dips at hour boundaries that "heal" later — operators chase ghosts.

| Grain | Buckets per day | Good for | Painful for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 seconds | 8,640 | Short-term ops charts | Long-range retention cost |
| 1 minute | 1,440 | Product analytics default | Sub-minute incident zoom |
| 5 minutes | 288 | Coarse SLOs, cheaper storage | Fast spike shape |
| 1 hour | 24 | Executive rollups | Almost any operational debug |

Many pipelines keep two grains: fine for recent hot data, rolled-up coarse for cold history — same idea as hot/cold storage, applied to aggregates.

## In an interview

Say what you materialize: "I will fold events into one-minute buckets keyed by the dimensions we chart, seal them after a short lateness grace period, and answer dashboard ranges by summing or merging those buckets — raw events stay available for re-aggregation when someone invents a new dimension." Expect questions on event time versus processing time, and on how unique counts over a window must merge HLLs rather than add per-bucket estimates. Naming watermark and grace period shows you have seen late data in production, not only in a diagram.
