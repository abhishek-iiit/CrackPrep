## Impression is a view with money attached

A content view can be wrong by a few percent and the product still works. An ad impression often cannot. Someone is paying per thousand impressions, a campaign budget caps spend, and a publisher gets paid on delivered volume. The write rate looks like view counting — high cardinality of creative ids, bursty traffic, hot campaigns — but the consumer of the number is a ledger, not a badge next to a thumbnail.

That changes the architecture before any sketch enters the picture. You still need the throughput tricks from 10.01 Counting At Scale and 10.02 View Counting At Scale: do not lock one row per impression event. You also need a path that can answer "how many billable impressions did creative X deliver between 14:00 and 15:00 UTC" with an auditable trail, which a lossy in-memory counter alone will never give you.

## Two pipelines sharing one event

The durable design almost always splits **ingestion** from **settlement**.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Emit an impression event once, with enough identity to dedupe">
    On render (or on a validated viewability signal), write an event: campaign id, creative id, placement, user or device id, timestamp, and a request id. Prefer append-only storage — a log or event bus — over updating a counter in the request path. The request returns after the event is accepted locally or acknowledged by the log; it does not wait for billing math.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply fraud and validity filters before the count that money sees">
    Bot scores, impossible geography, stacked iframes, below-the-fold never-viewable inventory — these drop or quarantine events. Filtering after a counter has already incremented forces expensive negative adjustments. Filtering on the stream before settlement keeps the money counter monotonic in the direction you want.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Dedupe inside a uniqueness window">
    Contracts often say "one countable impression per user per creative per N minutes" or similar. That is an exact membership question for the window, not a HyperLogLog estimate. For short windows and bounded keys, a Redis set or a Bloom filter of recent `(user, creative)` pairs can absorb the check — 04.13 Bloom Filters fits when a false positive only skips a countable impression (slight undercount), which is usually safer for the buyer than a false negative that double-charges. For strict contractual uniqueness, use an exact structure or a deterministic store keyed by the dedupe tuple.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Aggregate into time buckets for serving and billing">
    Roll valid events into per-creative, per-minute or per-hour counters — 10.06 Bucketed Time Window Aggregation — and keep the raw or filtered event log long enough to re-aggregate when definitions change. Real-time dashboards read the buckets; invoices read a settled daily aggregate produced by a batch job that can be replayed.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Approximate structures still belong on the **ops and product** side: "roughly how many impressions is this campaign pacing toward right now," unique reach estimates via HyperLogLog, heavy creative detection via 10.08 Count-Min Sketch. They do not replace the settlement counters.

<Callout type="warn">
Mixing "viewable impression," "rendered impression," and "billed impression" into one counter is a recurring incident class. Each definition has different filters and different contractual meaning. Name the metric in the schema (`impression_rendered`, `impression_viewable`, `impression_billable`) and never let a dashboard silently switch which one it sums.
</Callout>

## Capacity shape that shows up in interviews

Order-of-magnitude, not a benchmark: a large feed can produce tens of thousands of impression events per second at peak. If each event is a few hundred bytes on the wire and you retain raw events for 30–90 days for dispute, storage and scan cost dominate long before counter memory does. That is why derived buckets exist — so routine queries never scan the raw log — and why 10.07 Raw Events Vs Derived Analytics is part of this design rather than an afterthought.

| Path | Exactness | Latency | Typical consumer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Request-path buffer / Redis INCR | Best-effort | Milliseconds | Pacing UI, rate limits |
| Stream + filtered aggregates | High, replayable | Seconds to minutes | Ops dashboards |
| Daily settlement job | Audited | Hours | Invoices, partner reports |
| Reach / unique estimates (HLL) | Approximate | Seconds | Planning, frequency caps (soft) |

Frequency capping ("show this ad at most 3 times per user per day") sits between these rows. Soft caps can use sketches or eventually consistent counters; hard caps that affect spend need exact or strongly consistent per-user state, which does not scale the same way as global campaign totals — shard by user, not by campaign, for that state.

## In an interview

Separate money from chrome immediately: "I will append impression events, filter for fraud and viewability, dedupe by contract window, then aggregate into time buckets for dashboards and a replayable settlement job for billing — the request path never updates the invoice counter directly." Be ready to say where Bloom filters or exact sets enforce uniqueness, where HyperLogLog estimates reach, and why Count-Min Sketch helps find hot creatives without storing every key. The follow-up is usually fraud or double-counting: walk the idempotency key (`request_id` or `(user, creative, window)`) and state whether a false positive undercounts or overcounts under your choice of structure.
