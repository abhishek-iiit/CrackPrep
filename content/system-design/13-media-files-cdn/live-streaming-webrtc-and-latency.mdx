## Live is not VOD with a shorter file

VOD can buffer aggressively, pick a bitrate after a few seconds of observation, and hide jitter behind a large player buffer. Live cannot: every second of buffer you add is a second the viewer sits behind reality. A sports bettor, a auction bidder, and a viewer watching a recorded concert all say "live" and mean different glass-to-glass budgets — roughly sub-second, a few seconds, or 10–30 seconds respectively. The protocol choice is almost entirely a function of that budget and of how many concurrent viewers must see the same stream.

Classic HLS or DASH live encodes into segments of 2–6 seconds, publishes a playlist that lists a sliding window of those segments, and has the player fetch them over HTTP via a CDN. That path scales the way any CDN object scales — cheaply into the millions — and pays for it with latency usually measured in 10–30+ seconds end to end once encode, segment duration, playlist refresh, and player buffer are stacked. Low-latency HLS/DASH shortens segments (or uses chunked CMAF transfer) and shrinks the buffer; you can land in the 3–7 second range with careful packaging, still on HTTP and still CDN-friendly. WebRTC (and similar real-time media stacks) push media over UDP with aggressive jitter buffers measured in tens to hundreds of milliseconds — glass-to-glass under a second is realistic — but fan-out is no longer "put an object on a PoP."

## Where the latency budget goes

Glass-to-glass is the sum of contributions that do not care about your slide deck:

| Stage | Typical cost (order of magnitude) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capture + encode | ~100 ms–1 s | Software encode and large GOPs add more; hardware and short GOPs help |
| Packaging / segment duration | Dominant for HLS | A 6 s segment alone floors latency near that size before the player buffers more |
| Ingest transport | Tens–hundreds of ms | RTMP/SRT/WHIP to the origin; re-encode ladders add another pass |
| CDN / edge hop | Small per hop for HTTP; large if origin-shield misses | Live mid-segment often needs origin pull carefully tuned |
| Player buffer | Seconds for HLS; sub-second for WebRTC | Stability versus latency is the explicit trade |

If the product needs chat that reacts to the video in near lockstep, or a host who calls on a raised hand, a 20-second HLS delay makes the social layer feel broken even when video quality is fine. If the product is a keynote replayed to 500k viewers, that same delay is invisible and the CDN economics dominate.

## Three shapes of live delivery

<Steps>
  <Step title="Broadcaster ingest">
    Camera or encoder pushes to an ingest point (RTMP, SRT, or WHIP for WebRTC). That single contribution stream is transcoded into a ladder of renditions — the same idea as 13.05 Video Transcoding Pipeline, but under a live deadline instead of a batch queue.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Distribution choice">
    **HLS/DASH via CDN** for large fan-out and latency tolerance. **Low-latency CMAF/HLS** when a few seconds is acceptable and HTTP caching must remain. **WebRTC SFU** when sub-second matters and viewer count is hundreds to low thousands per session (more with a specialized real-time CDN).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Playback and adaptation">
    HTTP players adapt bitrate by switching segments (13.06 Adaptive Bitrate And CDN Decider). WebRTC adapts via congestion control and simulcast/SVC layers. Failure modes differ: HLS under stress shows rebuffering; WebRTC under stress shows freeze, freezes, or quality cliffs as layers drop.
  </Step>
</Steps>

An SFU (selective forwarding unit) receives one upstream and forwards selected layers to subscribers without mixing — far cheaper than a full MCU mix, still O(viewers) on the media plane. That is why "just use WebRTC for the football final" fails: you have reinvented a real-time CDN, and the bill and the ops surface follow.

<Callout type="note">
Hybrid designs are common: WebRTC for the interactive stage (host + guests), HLS for the audience of record. The audience watches a deliberately delayed CDN stream; only the people in the session need sub-second. Do not force one protocol to serve both roles unless the audience size fits the SFU.
</Callout>

## In an interview

Open with the budget, not the acronym: "I need to know glass-to-glass — under a second, a few seconds, or tens of seconds — and the concurrent viewer count; those two numbers pick HLS versus low-latency HTTP versus WebRTC." Be ready to walk the latency stack (segment duration and player buffer dominate HLS) and to say why WebRTC fan-out does not magically equal CDN fan-out. If asked how you would do a million-viewer sports stream with interactive hosts, name the hybrid: WebRTC on stage, HLS to the audience.
