## "Five nines" is not a strategy until someone owns the other 0.001%

Availability slogans without a measurement window, a success definition, and a consequence are theatre. An SLO (service level objective) is a target on a *service level indicator* — a metric that stands in for user happiness — over a time window: for example, 99.9% of authenticated API requests in 30 days return non-5xx within 300 ms. The gap between 100% and the SLO is the **error budget**: the amount of unreliability you have explicitly agreed to tolerate so that change can continue.

Without a budget, every incident argues for a freeze and every product asks for a feature. With a budget, burning it too fast is an automatic signal to slow releases; leaving it unspent is a signal you may be underinvesting in delivery. That is the point of the mechanism — not the decimal places on the poster.

## Pick indicators users would recognize

Good SLIs are close to user experience: availability of a read path, latency at a high percentile, freshness of a feed, success of a checkout commit. Bad SLIs are convenient for the system and invisible to the user: CPU idle, "process is up", disk free. A green CPU graph beside a broken checkout is how trust in ops dies.

| SLI type | Example | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability | Fraction of requests not 5xx / not timeout | Counting `4xx` as failures punishes clients; usually exclude them |
| Latency | Fraction of requests faster than threshold at a percentile | Average latency is a vanity metric; use histograms and a threshold |
| Quality / correctness | Fraction of results matching a checker | Harder to measure; worth it when silent wrong is worse than down |
| Freshness | Fraction of reads within N seconds of write | Critical for feeds, inventory, pricing |

Multi-endpoint services need care: one global SLO can be green while the payment route is on fire. Either weight by traffic carefully or define separate SLOs for critical journeys. 14.01 Observability For Distributed Systems is what feeds the indicators; the SLO is the policy on top.

## Windows, burn, and alerting

A 30-day rolling window is common for product SLOs; shorter windows detect acute burn. **Burn rate** asks how fast you are consuming budget relative to the pace that would exhaust it exactly at window end. Alerting on burn rate (multi-window, multi-burn-rate pages) beats alerting on every blip: a five-minute outage at high QPS can burn a month's 99.9% budget; a slow drip of errors can burn it quietly overnight.

<Formula label="Allowed failure fraction">error budget = 1 − SLO target</Formula>

At 99.9% over 30 days you may "spend" roughly 0.1% of requests as failures — on the order of 43 minutes of full outage if traffic were flat, which it never is; treat that conversion as intuition, not accounting. Real budgets are computed from the same request stream as the SLI.

<Callout type="note">
SLOs are not SLAs. An SLA is a contractual promise with credits and lawyers. An SLO is an internal engineering target. Publishing an ambitious SLA without an internal SLO and budget process is how you buy pager pain and refunds simultaneously.
</Callout>

## What the budget buys you

When budget remains, ship: features, refactors, experiments. When budget is exhausted (or burn alerts fire), prioritize reliability work — rollbacks, capacity, defect fixes — and raise the bar on risky deploys (14.04 Deployment And Migration Safety). The budget does not care whether the burn came from a bad release, an upstream dependency, or a load test left on; accounting may, but the user-facing SLI does not.

Dependencies complicate ownership: if your SLO includes a managed database's outage, you either absorb that risk in the budget, exclude that class of failure explicitly, or add dependency SLOs and composite targets. Pretending a dependency is 100% is writing a fiction into the objective.

## In an interview

Introduce SLOs as a product reliability contract with a measurable SLI, then define error budget as "the unreliability we can afford in exchange for change." Give one concrete example with a percentile latency or availability target and a window. Expect "what do you do when the budget is burned?" — answer: slow feature launch, focus on reliability work, tighten deploy controls — and "how do you alert?" — burn rate, not single failed requests. Distinguish SLO from SLA without being asked if you can.
