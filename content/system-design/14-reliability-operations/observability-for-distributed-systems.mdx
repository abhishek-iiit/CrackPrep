## You cannot SSH into a thousand replicas and eyeball stdout

A single process fails with a stack trace. A distributed request fails as a slow user, a `503`, or a correct response that skipped a side effect three services away. The machine that "owns" the bug may be healthy; the one that dropped the message may have already rotated its logs. Observability is the property that lets you ask new questions about production behavior from the data the system already emits — not a synonym for "we installed a dashboard."

Without it, every incident becomes archaeology: reproduce locally if you are lucky, ship guesswork if you are not. With the wrong shape of it — unbounded verbose logs, metrics with 50-dimensional labels, traces sampled to invisibility — you pay the cost of telemetry and still cannot answer "why was checkout slow for this customer at 14:02?"

## Three signals that answer different questions

| Signal | Answers | Weak at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Metrics** | How bad, how many, is it still happening? RED/USE aggregates, SLOs (14.02) | Explaining a single request; high-cardinality "why" |
| **Logs / events** | What did this service think it did? Errors, state transitions | Correlating across services without shared IDs; volume cost |
| **Traces** | Where did this request spend time across process boundaries? | Cheap fleet-wide aggregates; storage if you keep 100% forever |

Metrics are cheap aggregates: counters, gauges, histograms. Track request rate, error rate, and latency histograms (RED) per service, and utilization / saturation / errors (USE) per resource. Histograms matter for latency — averages hide the p99 that users feel (02.12 Tail Latency).

Logs earn their keep when they are **structured** (key-value fields, not free-text novels) and when every line that might join a cross-service story carries a **trace id** and **request id**. Debug-level logging in hot paths is how you DDOS your own logging pipeline during an outage — the moment you need logs most.

Traces stitch spans across services via context propagation (traceparent / baggage). Sampling is mandatory at scale: head-based sampling is simple and biased toward discarding rare failures; tail-based sampling keeps slow and error traces at higher rates and is usually what you want for incident work once volume hurts.

<Callout type="gotcha">
A metric label for `user_id` or full `url` turns a time series into a unique series per user or path — cardinality explosion that melts the metrics backend and quietly drops the data you thought you were storing. Keep labels bounded (service, route template, status class, region); put high-cardinality identity in logs and traces instead.
</Callout>

## Design telemetry into the request path

<Steps>
  <Step title="Propagate context at every hop">
    Gateways, workers, and async consumers must forward trace and request identifiers. A queue that drops headers creates a forest of orphan spans — useful metrics, useless traces.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Emit RED metrics at the edge of each service">
    One histogram for latency, counters for requests and errors, with low-cardinality labels. This feeds both dashboards and SLO burn alerts (14.02 SLOs And Error Budgets).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Log at decision boundaries">
    Authz deny, shed decision, retry, fallback to cache, "wrote to topic X" — the points where behavior branches. Not every function entry.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Trace the critical user journeys end to end">
    Checkout, login, publish-video — few journeys, high value. Confirm sampling keeps errors and high latency.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Async workflows need the same discipline with different topology: a producer span links to consumer spans via the message's trace context, or you accept that "the queue ate it" is invisible. 08.04 Queue Lag is a metric; without correlation ids on the job payload, lag does not tell you which user's work is stuck.

## What "good" looks like during an incident

Can you go from an SLO alert to a service, from the service to a spike in dependency latency, from a slow trace example to the span that dominates, from that span to logs for the same trace id — in minutes, without redeploying with more logging? If any hop requires a code change, observability was incomplete. 14.03 Incident Response assumes this ladder exists; without it, response degrades to rolling restarts and folklore.

<Tradeoff
  forTitle="Invest in traces + structured correlation when"

## In an interview

Define observability as the ability to explain novel failures from telemetry, then name the three pillars with different jobs: "Metrics tell me something is wrong; traces tell me where in the path; logs tell me what that service decided." Mention cardinality and context propagation unprompted — they are the usual follow-ups. For a design prompt, say which journey you would trace and which SLO metrics you would emit, rather than listing vendor product names.
