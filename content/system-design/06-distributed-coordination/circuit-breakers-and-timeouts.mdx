## The dependency that takes your service down with it

Service A calls service B. B slows from 5 ms to 2 seconds under load. A's threads pile up waiting; A's own callers time out; A's autoscaler adds pods that all wait on B; B's queue grows further. Nobody deployed a bug. The call graph amplified a slow dependency into a site-wide outage. Retries without budgets make it worse — every timed-out call becomes three more calls into the same fire (02.09 Retries Timeouts And Idempotency).

A **timeout** is the deadline on a single attempt: after N milliseconds you stop waiting and take a branch (fail, retry, degrade). A **circuit breaker** is a higher-level switch: after enough failures or timeouts, stop calling B for a cool-down period and fail fast (or serve a fallback) so A remains responsive and B gets room to recover. Timeouts answer "how long will I wait this time." Breakers answer "should I call at all right now."

06.01 Distributed Systems Foundations already warned that a timeout is not proof the work failed. Circuit breakers inherit that ambiguity: opening the breaker is a load-shedding decision under uncertainty, not a declaration that B is dead forever.

## Timeouts are budgets, not vibes

Pick timeouts from the latency budget, not from folklore.

- Work **backward from the client's SLO**. If the user-facing p99 budget is 300 ms and you have two sequential dependencies, each cannot honestly get a 500 ms timeout.
- Separate **connect**, **request**, and **total** deadlines when the client library allows it — a hung TCP connect should not burn the whole budget the way a slow handler does.
- Prefer **deadlines that propagate** (a remaining budget header or context deadline) over stacked fixed timeouts, so a deep call chain cannot accumulate "500 ms each" into multi-second waits.
- Size retries with **backoff and a retry budget** (02.09): a timeout without a retry policy is incomplete; retries without a budget recreate the thundering herd.

Order-of-magnitude anchors help interviews: same-datacenter RPC often lives in the low single-digit to tens of milliseconds when healthy; cross-region hops commonly cost tens to a couple hundred milliseconds. A timeout just above the healthy p99 plus a little headroom is a starting point — not a number copied from another company's blog.

## Circuit breaker states

Most breakers are a small state machine:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Closed — calls flow">
    Failures and timeouts are counted (consecutive failures, or a failure-rate window). Below threshold, the dependency is treated as healthy.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Open — calls short-circuit">
    Once the threshold trips, subsequent calls fail immediately (or hit a fallback) without touching the network. The sick dependency stops receiving traffic from this client. After a configured open duration, the breaker moves to half-open.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Half-open — probe">
    A limited number of trial calls are allowed through. If they succeed, the breaker closes; if they fail, it opens again. This avoids flipping open forever and avoids dumping the full load the instant the cool-down ends.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Breakers are usually **per dependency** (and often per endpoint or per shard), not one global switch for the whole process. A payment service being down should not open the breaker for the read-only profile store.

| Control | Protects | Typical mis-tune |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timeout | Thread/connection pools from unbounded waits | Too long → cascades; too short → false failure under load |
| Retry | Transient blips | Unbounded retries → amplify outage |
| Circuit breaker | Caller and callee from sustained failure | Too sensitive → flap; too dull → cascade anyway |
| Bulkhead / pool limit | One dependency exhausting all workers | Shared pools across dependencies |
| Load shed (02.13) | Overload admission | Shedding the wrong priority traffic |

<Callout type="gotcha">
Failing open with an empty fallback can look like success to metrics while serving wrong answers — cached emptiness, default permissions, zero balances. Pair breakers with explicit degraded-mode behavior and alerts on open state duration, not only on error rate.
</Callout>

## What the breaker cannot do

It cannot make B correct. It cannot replace idempotency on the write path. It cannot save you if the "fallback" is another call into the same failing cluster. And a breaker that keys only on HTTP 5xx will miss the slow-B failure mode unless timeouts (or hedged-request failure) count as failures too — latency-induced cascades are the usual reason you wanted the breaker.

Bulkheads — separate thread pools, connection pools, or containers per dependency — complement breakers: even with a slow call that has not yet tripped the breaker, a dedicated pool caps how much of A B can consume. Timeouts + pools + breakers + retry budgets are one design; any one alone is a partial fix.

## In an interview

Describe the cascade in one breath: "B slows, A waits, retries multiply, A falls over." Then place three controls: timeout from the SLO budget, retry with backoff and a budget, breaker that opens on timeout/error rate and serves a named fallback. Mention bulkheads if they ask how one dependency cannot consume all workers. Name the failure mode: breakers that ignore slowness, or fallbacks that look healthy while wrong. Tie it to partial failure (06.01) — open means "we stopped waiting," not "we know B rolled back."
