## "NoSQL" names what you rejected, not what you picked

"NoSQL" is a negative definition — everything that isn't a relational database sharing one query language and one consistency model. That's not a design decision, it's the absence of one. Key-value stores, document stores, wide-column stores, and graph databases have almost nothing in common with each other beyond declining to be a relational table. Reach for "NoSQL" as an answer and you haven't answered anything; you still have to pick, from four or five families with incompatible strengths, the one whose access pattern actually matches your workload.

Getting that pick wrong is not "a bit slower" — it's often "the query I need doesn't exist." Store a social graph in a document store and multi-hop traversal is slow, because the engine was never built to walk relationships. Store a write-heavy event stream in a database designed around multi-row transactions and joins, and every insert fights the database for guarantees nobody asked for. Getting the category wrong on day one usually means a rewrite rather than a tuning pass, because the data ends up laid out on disk for a query that isn't the one you're actually asking.

## The question that comes before the family

Before picking among key-value, document, wide-column, and graph, ask whether you need to leave relational at all. A relational database (03.01 Relational Database Design) scales further than its reputation suggests (03.10 Relational Database Scaling) and gives you ad hoc queries, joins, and multi-row transactions for free — every NoSQL family charges extra for at least one of those, or drops it. The honest reasons to leave are specific: the access pattern is fixed and known well enough to denormalize around, the write volume or dataset size has outgrown what a single primary and its read replicas can hold, the data itself doesn't fit rows well — deeply nested, sparse, or graph-shaped — or you need to trade consistency for availability at a scale where CAP (06.04 CAP And PACELC) actually bites.

## Match the shape of the query to the shape of the store

Each family is fast at one query shape and slow at almost everything else. That's not a limitation to work around — it's the entire reason the family exists.

| Family | Strongest at | Weak at | Canonical system |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Key-value | Single-key get/put at very high throughput and low latency | Anything that isn't "give me the value for this exact key" | Redis, DynamoDB used as KV |
| Document | Nested, variable-shape records fetched mostly whole, with secondary indexes | Multi-document transactions, deep cross-document joins | MongoDB, Couchbase |
| Wide-column | Sustained high-volume writes with a known row-key access pattern and sparse, wide rows | Ad hoc queries on non-key columns, cross-row aggregation | Cassandra, Bigtable, HBase |
| Graph | Multi-hop traversal — friend-of-friend, fraud rings, permission inheritance | Full-dataset scans and aggregation | Neo4j |
| Columnar (analytical) | Aggregating a few columns across billions of rows | Point lookups and row-level updates | Redshift, ClickHouse, BigQuery |

04.02 Document Stores vs Key-Value Stores, 04.03 Columnar Stores vs Wide-Column Stores, and 04.04 Graph Database Decision Boundary each take one of these boundaries apart in more detail — this lesson is the map, those are the close-ups.

<Callout type="note">
Most production systems don't pick one NoSQL family and use it everywhere. A typical stack keeps the system of record relational, caches hot reads in a key-value store, and indexes a search-specific view separately — one store per access pattern, not one store for the whole system. The decision this lesson describes gets made once per access pattern, not once per company.
</Callout>

## In an interview

Name the access pattern before the store. Saying "I'll use DynamoDB" before you've said what you're querying by is a guess dressed up as a decision. Say instead: "most of the traffic is a get-by-id at very high QPS, so I want a key-value store" — that's reasoning an interviewer can follow. Expect the follow-up "why not just use Postgres for this," and have a concrete answer ready: usually scale, shape, or availability, stated with a number or a query pattern attached, not "NoSQL is more scalable" as a slogan. Be ready, too, to say which consistency guarantee you're giving up and where — every one of these families gets its performance from relaxing something a relational database gives you by default.
