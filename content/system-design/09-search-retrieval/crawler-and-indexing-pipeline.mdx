## The web does not push; you have to pull carefully

An internal product catalog can emit change events into the index (09.10 Search Index Synchronization). The open web does not. Pages appear, move, and vanish without notifying anyone. A crawler discovers URLs, fetches content under politeness constraints, turns HTML into indexable documents, and only then feeds the same indexing machinery a private corpus would use. The hard part is not "download pages" — it is doing that continuously without DDoSing origins, without indexing near-duplicates as distinct documents, and without letting a poisoned or expired page sit in results for weeks.

This pipeline is asynchronous by nature (08.01 Delegation And Async Work). Discovery and fetch run on their own clocks; indexing freshness for the public web is measured in minutes to days depending on site importance, not in the seconds a CDC consumer targets for a shopping cart.

## The stages from frontier to postings

<Steps>
  <Step title="Seed and discover">
    Start from seed URLs and sitemaps; extract outbound links from fetched pages to grow the frontier. Discovery policy decides breadth versus depth — news homepages versus deep archival URLs — and is the first place crawl budget gets spent or wasted.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Prioritize the frontier">
    Not every URL is equal. Score by estimated change rate, historical traffic, site importance, and recency of last fetch. A priority queue (or sharded queues) holds millions to billions of URLs; the scheduler pops what is worth fetching next under a global crawl budget.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fetch under politeness rules">
    Per-host rate limits, robots.txt and crawl-delay, concurrency caps, and backoff on errors. One slow or angry origin must not stall the whole crawler — isolate by host and fail soft. Retries need jitter and a budget (02.09 Retries Timeouts And Idempotency).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Parse and extract">
    Convert bytes into a canonical document: title, body text, language, canonical URL, outbound links, and structured fields when present. Boilerplate stripping matters; navigation chrome indexed as content destroys precision later.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Dedupe and canonicalize">
    Near-duplicate detection (simhash, shingles) and URL canonicalization collapse mirrors, tracking-param variants, and printer-friendly twins into one document id. Without this step the index balloons and ranking signal dilutes across clones.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Index and schedule revisit">
    Emit the document into the indexing pipeline that builds inverted (and optionally vector) structures. Record a revisit schedule — volatile hubs more often than static docs — so the frontier is not only discovery but refresh.
  </Step>
</Steps>

| Concern | What goes wrong if ignored | Typical control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Politeness | Origin blocks you or legal/trust damage | Per-host QPS caps, robots.txt |
| Crawl budget | Waste fetches on junk; miss important updates | Priority scores, site quotas |
| Duplicates | Index bloat, split ranking signals | Canonical URL + near-dup hashing |
| Content quality | Spam and thin pages pollute top-k | Spam scores, link/graph signals at index time |
| Freshness | Stale results for news and prices | Change-rate predictors, sitemap lastmod |

## Crawl budget is a product decision wearing ops clothes

Every day the crawler can fetch a finite number of pages — hardware, bandwidth, and politeness all cap it. That budget must be allocated: refresh the head of the web that users actually query, explore enough of the long tail to stay useful, and leave margin for spikes when a major site publishes. Treating the frontier as FIFO is how you spend a week recrawling low-value archives while a homepage users hit hourly goes stale.

For **site search** over your own properties the same pipeline shrinks: sitemaps and CMS publish hooks replace open discovery, politeness is mostly self-inflicted rate limiting, and dedupe is narrower. Do not paste a web-scale crawler design into an interview about indexing a company's help center — say what you drop and why.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Canonicalization bugs are silent poison. If `https://example.com/page` and `https://example.com/page?utm_source=x` both become distinct documents, clicks and links split, BM25-style length and frequency stats skew, and users see near-identical results stacked. Normalize aggressively at extract time; do not hope ranking will "prefer the right one."
</Callout>

## Indexing is a second pipeline, not a function call

Fetched documents land in object storage or a blob store, then workers tokenize, analyze, and push into shard-local indexes (09.11 Search Index Sharding). Separating crawl from index lets each scale on different bottlenecks: crawl is network- and politeness-bound; indexing is CPU- and disk-bound. A backlog between them is normal — measure document age from fetch to searchable the same way you measure primary-to-index lag in 09.10 Search Index Synchronization.

Deletes and soft-404s need an explicit path: a fetch that finds gone content must remove or tombstone the document, or the corpus accumulates ghosts exactly like a missed delete in CDC sync.

## In an interview

Frame it as a budgeted pipeline: "Discovery fills a prioritized frontier; fetch respects per-host limits; parse, dedupe, then index — crawl and index scale separately." Ask whether the corpus is open web or first-party; that single clarification collapses half the design. Expect questions on politeness, duplicates, and how a deleted page leaves the index. Name crawl budget allocation before listing tools — interviewers are listening for whether you understand scarcity, not whether you remember a MapReduce paper title.
